																<p class="u-uppercase"><time datetime="2019-03-11T10:00:29+00:00">11 Mar 2019</time></p>

													<p>In mid-February, Kaspersky Lab received a request for incident response from one of its clients. The individual who initially reported the issue to our client refused to disclose the origin of the indicator that they shared. What we do know is that it was a screenshot from one of the client&#8217;s internal computers taken on February 11 while an employee was apparently browsing through his emails. In addition, the anonymous source added that the screenshot was transferred to a C2 using a stealer dubbed &#8216;Predator&#8217;.</p>
<p>As soon as the client contacted us, we started conducting a full investigation into the infected machine, including memory dumps, event logs, environment indicators from the network and so on and so forth. Finding very little information about this tool, we decided that seeing as how we&#8217;d already dived into the stealer, we might as well share some of our main findings in case other incidents occur in the future. The purpose of this blogpost is to enumerate the Predator stealer&#8217;s versions, technical features, indicators and Yara rule signatures, to help monitor and detect new samples, and to provide general information about its owners&#8217; activities.</p>
<p>As well as all the information we collected from the client, we went the extra mile and contacted a source who had previously analyzed Predator. This source was <a target="_blank" href="https://twitter.com/fumik0_" rel="noopener noreferrer">@Fumik0_</a>, a French malware researcher who analyzed versions 2.3.5 and 2.3.7 in his <a target="_blank" href="https://fumik0.com/2018/10/15/predator-the-thief-in-depth-analysis-v2-3-5/" rel="noopener noreferrer">blog</a> just a few months ago (October 2018).</p>
<p>He joined Ido Naor, a principal security researcher at Kaspersky Lab and together they compiled a full analysis of the new versions of &#8216;Predator the thief&#8217;.</p>
<p>The blog was apparently so influential that the owners of the stealer decided to contact Fumik0 via <a target="_blank" href="https://twitter.com/Alexupi1/status/1051876412142100482" rel="noopener noreferrer">Twitter</a>. An account named Alexuiop1337 claiming to be the owner of Predator is also active and has been responding to Fumik0&#8217;s discoveries <a target="_blank" href="https://twitter.com/fumik0_/status/1096344351490404352" rel="noopener noreferrer">until fairly recently</a>.</p>
<h2><strong>Predator the thief</strong></h2>
<p>Predator is a data stealer developed by Russian-speaking individuals. It&#8217;s being sold cheaply on Russian forums and has been detected many times in the wild. Although detection is successful with previous versions, its owners are rapidly adapting by generating FUD (Fully UnDetectable) samples every few days. The owners are not responsible for the victim attack vector and are only selling the builder. For a small additional payment they can also generate an administration panel for customers. The newest samples were exposed on their Telegram group; however, the links only redirect to a little-known AV aggregator which we don&#8217;t have access to. We&#8217;re currently tracking the samples&#8217; hashes and waiting for triggers to show up.</p>

<h2><strong>From v2 to v3</strong></h2>
<p>Predator, as a stealer, is considered simple and cheap. It&#8217;s good for attacking individuals and small businesses, but as far as large companies go, protection solutions and response teams can detect and remove its activity in a relatively short amount of time.</p>
<p>That said, the owners of Predator are very business oriented. They&#8217;re constantly updating their software, attempting to extend features and adjusting to client requirements and are generally not that aggressive when it comes to disclosure/analysis of their tool.</p>
<h4 id="obfuscation">Obfuscation</h4>
<p>Predator&#8217;s owners decided to obfuscate most of its code with a number of simple techniques. XOR, Base64, Substitutions, Stack strings and more are being used to hide API methods, Folder paths, Register keys, the C2 server/Admin panel and so on.</p>
<p>We sketched a flow chart for one of the obfuscation techniques. A large chunk of code boiled down to one Windows API call, which we see as a bit like overkill considering the fact that other techniques can be applied to strip the obfuscation.</p>

<h4 id="the-false-keylogger-feature">The false keylogger feature</h4>
<p>The owners of Predator list keylogger capabilities among its features, though a closer inspection of the code reveals that no keylogging is carried out. The behavior we captured is clearly that of a clipboard stealer. The functionality includes a crawler that checks if the clipboard contains data, grabs it and places it in a dedicated file the stealer owners have named &#8216;information.log&#8217;.</p>
<h4 id="thief-logs">Thief logs</h4>
<p>Diving into the file discussed in the clipboard stealer section above, we saw drastic changes from previous versions. The information logger is perhaps the most important collector of Predator. It stores all the tasks performed by the stealer on the victim machine.</p>

<h2><strong>Sale point (Russian forums)</strong></h2>
<p>We found a very active seller of Predator on a forum called VLMI. It appears the main language on VLMI is Russian and the content mainly revolves around cyberattacks. <p><a target="_blank" href="https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2019/03/11085426/190311-predatory-tale-7.png" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="magnificImage"><img decoding="async" src="https://media.kasperskycontenthub.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2019/03/11085426/190311-predatory-tale-7.png" style="max-height:60vh" alt="" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-81520" /></a></p>
